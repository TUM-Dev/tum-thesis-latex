\chapter{Evaluation}\label{chapter:evaluation}
This chapter evaluates the approach presented in
\autoref{chapter:approach} in two studies. The first examines how the
inputs to LLM-based seed generation should be configured, varying the
starting corpus and the scope of the \ac{CoI} and measuring the resulting
seed corpora in terms of both effectiveness and the resources they consume.
The second takes the configuration selected there and evaluates it
end-to-end, comparing the augmented corpus against the project's original
corpus in fuzzing campaigns targeting known regression bugs. Six subjects
are used to select the configuration; the fuzzing study adds a further six
held-out subjects, so that the end-to-end results can be read separately
for projects that did and did not inform that choice.
\autoref{sec:eval:setup} describes the subjects and the shared
experimental infrastructure, \autoref{sec:eval:seedgen} and
\autoref{sec:eval:fuzzing} report the two studies, and
\autoref{sec:eval:threats} discusses threats to validity.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval:setup}
\subsection{Subjects}\label{sec:eval:subjects}
Our subjects are drawn from the BugOSS benchmark by Kim and Hong~\cite{kimBugOssBenchmarkRealworld2024}, a collection of
reproducible real-world regression bugs in OSS-Fuzz projects. For each bug it
provides the bug-inducing commit (BIC), the bug-fixing commit (BFC), a
bug-revealing input, the locations of the bug and of the fix, and scripts
to build the respective project. The BIC is what makes the benchmark
suitable here, as it defines the change from which the \ac{CoI} is
computed. BugOSS is furthermore the benchmark used by the prior work our
\ac{CoI} computation is taken from, which keeps the cost of reusing that
tooling low. When we refer to a project such as libhtp in the remainder
of this chapter, we mean that project at its BIC, where the respective
bug has just been introduced.

Of the 21 projects in BugOSS, nine were excluded. Five fail a
precondition of our setup. \emph{arrow} is excluded because its defect is
not related to memory access and thus falls outside the bug class we
address, for which our metrics presuppose a sanitizer-detectable memory
error at a crash site. \emph{grok} is excluded because its BIC is no
longer available on GitHub, so no \ac{CoI} can be derived. \emph{gdal} is
excluded because its bitcode is too large for the static analysis, which
terminates out of memory. \emph{aspell} and \emph{file} are excluded
because their harnesses read configuration files supplied outside the
seed corpus; as the \ac{LLM} never observes these files, the augmentation
arms would be handicapped by an artifact of the harness rather than by
the property under study. 
\emph{openh264} is excluded because its BIC consists solely of removals.
The \ac{CoI} computation we depend on takes the lines Git classifies as
added in the BIC as its slicing criterion, and this set is empty for this
project. Admitting it would require deviating from the procedure applied
to every other subject.
The remaining three exclusions, \emph{libarchive}, \emph{exiv2}, and
\emph{leptonica}, were made to keep the evaluation
within budget; these four were not selected against a systematic
criterion. This leaves twelve subjects, six of which are also used to
select the configuration in \autoref{sec:eval:seedgen}; the split is
carried through every table and plot in \autoref{sec:eval:fuzzing}.

\subsection{Infrastructure and Configuration}
All experiments were carried out on a single Fujitsu CELSIUS M770
workstation running Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS (kernel 6.8.0). The machine is
equipped with a 6-core Intel Xeon W-2133 (Skylake, 12 hardware threads,
\SIrange{1.2}{3.9}{\giga\hertz}, \SI{8.25}{\mebi\byte} L3),
\SI{64}{\gibi\byte} of RAM, and an NVMe SSD holding all target builds and
corpora. Targets are not built on the host: each subject is compiled
inside its OSS-Fuzz build container, so the toolchain, sanitiser
configuration, and harness definitions are those the project uses
upstream and vary between subjects. Fuzzing campaigns are likewise
executed in OSS-Fuzz runner containers, each campaign pinned to a dedicated hardware thread. We use AFL++~5.03a (stable) for all campaigns.

Seed generation uses DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731~\cite{deepseek-aiDeepSeekV4HighlyEfficient2026},
a \num{304}-billion-parameter MoE model released under the MIT license,
accessed through the provider's hosted \ac{API} rather than served locally.
The model was selected as the strongest openly licensed model with agentic
capabilities available at the lowest cost per token at the time the
experiments were conducted. All requests were issued
during August~2026 against the \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} endpoint;
since hosted endpoints may be updated without notice, the results reported
here are tied to that period. Following the model's recommended settings for
agentic use, we sample with a temperature of \num{1.0} and $\text{top-}p$ of
\num{0.95}, at the \texttt{high} reasoning effort level.

\subsection{Statistical Procedure}\label{sec:eval:statistics}

Raw measurements are not directly comparable across projects because their
numerical scales and attainable values can differ substantially. The analysis
therefore proceeds in two stages: configurations are compared within each
project, where they share the same target, and the resulting project-level
outcomes are aggregated across projects using ranks and normalised scores.
This follows established practice for cross-benchmark evaluation in fuzzing and
search-based software engineering~\cite{demsar2006,metzman2021,klees2018,arcuri2014,schloegel2024}.

\paragraph{Effect and significance.}
For each comparison, statistical significance, effect size, and practical effect
are considered separately. Statistical significance is assessed at
$\alpha=0.05$, where $\alpha$ denotes the threshold for rejecting the null
hypothesis. The \emph{effect size} quantifies the magnitude and direction of a
difference independently of the metric's scale, whereas the \emph{practical
effect} expresses the difference in the original metric units, e.g., as a
difference in coverage, time, or corpus size. Thus, statistical significance
indicates evidence for a difference, while effect size and practical effect
describe its magnitude and practical relevance.

The attainable significance level is constrained by the small number of
campaigns and projects; consequently, even complete separation of the observed
outcomes may not yield $p<0.05$. A non-significant result is therefore not
interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Effect estimates and their confidence
intervals are reported regardless of significance to convey the observed
direction and magnitude of the differences.

\paragraph{Within-project comparisons.}
The first stage evaluates configurations within each project.
Each \emph{arm} denotes one experimental configuration being compared. Each arm
is evaluated through repeated campaigns with independent random seeds. Because
the number of campaigns is small and the resulting distributions need not
satisfy parametric assumptions, rank-based tests are used. Medians are reported
as descriptive summaries because they are robust to extreme observations.

For two arms, the Mann--Whitney $U$ test compares the independent sets of
campaign outcomes. The effect size is quantified using the Vargha--Delaney
$\hat{A}*{12}$ statistic. After orienting each metric such that larger values
indicate better performance, $\hat{A}*{12}$ denotes the probability that a
randomly selected run from the first arm outperforms a randomly selected run
from the second; $\hat{A}_{12}=0.5$ indicates equal stochastic dominance.
Bootstrap confidence intervals are reported for the estimate. The median
difference is reported alongside the test and effect size to quantify the
practical effect in the original metric units.

For three arms, the Kruskal--Wallis test is used as an \emph{omnibus test},
i.e., a single test of whether there is evidence that at least one arm differs
from the others. If significant, Dunn's test is applied to the three pairwise
contrasts, with Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Across-project aggregation.}
The within-project comparisons provide project-specific results, but do not
by themselves provide a summary across the benchmark. The second stage
therefore treats the \emph{project} as the matched unit of analysis, with each
project contributing one summary value per arm. Metrics are first oriented so
that larger values indicate better performance. Two complementary summaries
are then computed for each project.

The \emph{within-project rank} orders the configurations according to their
project-level median, with rank $1$ assigned to the best-performing
configuration. The \emph{average rank} is the mean of these ranks across
projects and therefore captures consistency across projects. In contrast, the
\emph{normalised score} expresses a configuration's project-level median
relative to the best-performing configuration on that project, which receives
a score of $100$. The \emph{average normalised score} is the mean of these
scores across projects. Unlike ranks, normalised scores retain information
about the relative magnitude of differences. Reporting both summaries
therefore distinguishes consistent advantages from advantages driven by
larger gains on individual projects.

These project-level summaries also provide the paired observations required
for statistical testing across projects. For two-arm comparisons, the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test is applied to the paired project-level differences,
i.e., the difference between the two configurations' median outcomes within
each project. For three arms, the Friedman test provides the corresponding
omnibus test for the matched project blocks; significant results are followed
by the Nemenyi procedure for pairwise comparisons.

\section{Seed Generation Study}\label{sec:eval:seedgen}
In the first study, evaluating how the inputs to LLM-based seed generation
are configured, we seek to answer the following research questions. 

\begin{description}
  \item[\researchq{1.1} (starting corpus)] To what extent does the choice of
    starting corpus (none, distilled, or original) affect the effectiveness
    and resource requirements of LLM-based seed generation for
    change-targeted fuzzing?
  \item[\researchq{1.2} (\ac{CoI} scope)] How do effectiveness and resource
    requirements change when the \ac{CoI} is bounded rather than taken in
    full?
\end{description}

We use \emph{effectiveness} to refer to the reachability of the crash site of the
generated seeds, their coverage of the affected code, and the resulting
corpus size; and \emph{resource requirements} to refer to token consumption
and wall-clock time, split into pre-augmentation and augmentation time.

We vary one factor at a time. \rqref{1.1} is answered at a fixed \ac{CoI}
scope; \rqref{1.2} is then answered at the starting corpus selected by
\rqref{1.1}, so that the recommended combination is a configuration we
measure rather than one we infer. This ordering keeps the study within
budget, at the cost of characterizing the effect of \ac{CoI} scope only
for the selected starting corpus. Combining both research questions, we
determine which starting corpus and which \ac{CoI} scope yields the most
useful seed corpus per unit of cost, under this restriction.

\subsection{Subjects}\label{sec:eval:seedgen:subjects}
This study uses six of the twelve subjects. Two considerations motivate
the restriction. First, this is the study in which the configuration is
selected, so every subject it uses informs the result. Confining it to a
subset leaves six projects that played no part in that choice, allowing
the campaigns in \autoref{sec:eval:fuzzing} to be read separately for
subjects that did and did not influence it. Second, the cost per subject
is asymmetric: this study runs four arms with 5 repetitions each, whereas the
fuzzing study constructs each corpus once and varies only the campaigns.
The metered token budget is therefore consumed almost entirely here.

Within these constraints the subset was chosen to span the dimensions
along which seed generation is expected to vary. The primary
stratification is input format, since an \ac{LLM}'s ability to synthesize
valid inputs plausibly depends on whether the format exposes
human-readable structure; the subset is balanced evenly between mixed
text-binary formats and purely binary ones. It further covers three
classes of memory-safety defect, program sizes spanning an order of
magnitude, and both seed-scarce and seed-rich starting conditions. These
six subjects recur in \autoref{sec:eval:fuzzing} as the tuning subjects.

\section{Fuzzing Study}\label{sec:eval:fuzzing}
In the second study, evaluating the resulting corpus in an end-to-end
fuzzing campaign, we compare the LLM-augmented corpus produced by the
configuration selected in \rq{1.1} and \rq{1.2} against the project's
original corpus. We seek to answer the following research questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[\researchq{2.1} (reliability)] How often do campaigns seeded with
    each corpus expose the target regression bug within the campaign budget?
  \item[\researchq{2.2} (generalization)] Does the detection rate on the
    subjects used to select the configuration differ from that on the held-out
    subjects?
  \item[\researchq{2.3} (efficiency)] How does the expected
    time-to-exposure of the target bug within the campaign budget differ between the two seed
    corpora?
\end{description}

Together, the research questions establish whether the gains observed in
the seed generation study translate into an end-to-end fuzzing campaign,
and whether they hold on subjects that played no part in selecting the
configuration.

\subsection{Subjects}\label{sec:eval:fuzzing:subjects}
This study uses all twelve subjects introduced in
\autoref{sec:eval:subjects}. Six are the subjects on which the
configuration was selected in \autoref{sec:eval:seedgen}; the remaining
six played no part in that selection and serve to expose any overfitting
of the configuration to the selection set. We mark this split in every
per-subject table and plot, so that it remains visible whether an
observed effect is confined to the subjects that informed the
configuration.

Aggregate figures and statistical tests are computed over the full set of
twelve; the split is not treated as a factor. On four of the six held-out
subjects neither corpus exposed the bug within the budget, leaving too
few informative subjects for a separate comparison.

\subsection{Procedure}\label{sec:eval:fuzzing:procedure}
Each subject is fuzzed under two arms: seeded with the project's
original corpus, and seeded with the LLM-augmented corpus produced by
the configuration selected in \autoref{sec:eval:seedgen}. Corpus
construction is performed once per subject, so the only source of
randomness within an arm is the fuzzing campaign itself. We do not
include a compute-matched control that grants the original corpus
additional fuzzing time equal to the generation wall-clock; this is out
of scope here and is carried to \autoref{sec:threats}.

We use a campaign budget of \SI{8}{\hour}, the longest campaign that
still fits an overnight CI/CD
run~\cite{kloosterEffectivenessScalabilityFuzzing2022}. This is shorter
than the \SI{24}{\hour} convention common in fuzzer
evaluations~\cite{kleesEvaluatingFuzzTesting2018}, and the trade-off runs
in a direction that favours the technique under test: a short budget
rewards seeds that pay off early, which is precisely what per-commit regression targeted corpus
augmentation is designed to produce. We report this explicitly rather
than treating \SI{8}{\hour} as neutral, and revisit it in
\autoref{sec:threats}.

Every arm is run for ten independent campaigns per subject, each with a
distinct fuzzer seed and no state shared between runs. A campaign counts
as a reproduction only when the crash matches the target regression:
BugOSS supplies the bug-revealing input together with the bug and fix
locations, and we confirm a match against the sanitiser report and
faulting location rather than counting arbitrary crashes.

Campaigns that reach the budget without reproducing the bug have no
\ac{TTE}. We treat these as right-censored at \SI{8}{\hour} rather than
discarding them or imputing a value, following Magma, which applies
survival analysis to fuzzing campaigns in order to handle the
measurements left missing by trials in which a fuzzer never reaches the
bug~\cite{hazimehMagmaGroundTruthFuzzing2020}. Each subject and arm is
summarised by the \ac{RMST} at the \SI{8}{\hour} horizon: the average
time for which the bug survives undetected over a campaign capped at
\SI{8}{\hour}, computed as the area under the Kaplan--Meier estimate.
\ac{RMST} is defined for every subject, including those where fewer than
half the campaigns reproduce the bug and the median \ac{TTE} does not
exist. All reported \ac{RMST} values are relative to the \SI{8}{\hour}
budget and are not comparable to figures obtained under a different
horizon.

\paragraph{Unit of analysis.}
Campaigns are not pooled across subjects. Corpus construction is
performed once per subject, so the ten campaigns within a subject are
repeated measurements of one constructed corpus rather than independent
samples of the technique, and the subject is the unit of analysis for
every cross-subject statement. Within a subject the ten campaigns are
independent and are compared directly. \ac{RMST} is the primary outcome
of this study; the reproduction rate is the endpoint of the same survival
curve and is reported as a reliability view of it rather than as
stand-alone evidence.

\paragraph{Reproduction rate.}
Reproduction rates are reported per subject as the fraction of campaigns
exposing the bug, together with the mean rate per arm. Within a subject
the evidence is a $2\times2$ table of campaigns, which we test with
Fisher's exact test, adjusted with Holm's step-down procedure over the
twelve subjects. Across subjects we take one paired difference per
subject and test it with an exact two-sided sign test. A campaign's
outcome is a single bit, so a subject's difference is a gap between two
proportions out of ten and only its direction is safe to use; a
rank-based test would treat a one-campaign gap as commensurable with a
nine-campaign gap. The accompanying effect size is the Hodges--Lehmann
estimate of the median paired difference across subjects.

\paragraph{Time to exposure.}
Within each subject we compare the two arms' Kaplan--Meier curves with a
two-sided log-rank test over the censored campaign times, again adjusted
with Holm's procedure over the subjects for which a test is defined. We
report permutation $p$-values obtained from \num{100000} arm-label
shuffles rather than the asymptotic $\chi^2$ approximation, which ten
campaigns per arm do not justify. On four subjects neither arm exposed
the bug in any campaign; the two curves are then identical, no hazard
ratio exists, and we report no test rather than a $p$ of \num{1.00},
which would read as evidence of equivalence. Across subjects we take the
per-subject difference in \ac{RMST} and test these paired differences
with an exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test, reporting the Hodges--Lehmann
estimate of the median paired difference as the effect size in hours.

\paragraph{Why the within-subject test is not a rank test over campaign
times.}
Klees et al.\ recommend the Mann--Whitney U test for comparing fuzzing
configurations~\cite{kleesEvaluatingFuzzTesting2018}. We depart from that
recommendation within subjects because of the observation window. A
campaign that had not exposed the bug when the \SI{8}{\hour} window
closed yields a lower bound on its exposure time, not a measurement, and
a rank test consumes the numbers it is given and therefore reads that
bound as an exposure \emph{at} the window. The resulting error is a bias
rather than a loss of power, and it is largest where the data are
thinnest: for \emph{readstat}, where one campaign of twenty exposed the
bug at all, Mann--Whitney returns $p = \num{0.368}$ while a log-rank on
the same campaigns returns $p = \num{1.000}$ --- correctly, since a
single exposure cannot separate two survival curves. The log-rank
consumes $(\text{time},\ \text{event})$ pairs instead, so a censored
campaign contributes to the risk set at every instant before the window
and to the exposures at none. The cross-subject test is unaffected by
this argument and remains a Wilcoxon signed-rank test: its inputs are the
per-subject \ac{RMST} values, which already account for the censoring.

\paragraph{Assumptions.}
No distributional form is assumed for the time to exposure. The log-rank
statistic depends on the event times only through their order within the
pooled risk sets, its $p$-value is obtained by permuting arm labels
rather than from an asymptotic approximation, and the reported effect is
a difference in \ac{RMST}, a functional of the Kaplan--Meier estimator.
Censoring is administrative at the common \SI{8}{\hour} window and is
therefore independent of the search process by construction.
Proportional hazards is not required for the validity of the test and is
not assumed; no pooled hazard ratio is reported. The cross-subject tests
are reported for consistency with the comparisons made elsewhere in this
evaluation, but they have little power at twelve subjects: subjects whose
difference is exactly zero are dropped as ties, and with the numbers of
non-tied subjects observed here neither test could have reached
$\alpha = \num{0.05}$ unless nearly every remaining subject agreed in
direction. We return to this in \autoref{sec:threats}.

\subsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{The effect is real in places and absent or reversed in others.}
The augmented corpus was faster on five subjects and slower on three, and
in four of the eight subjects where a test is possible, the difference is
large enough to survive correction --- in one of them against
augmentation. This is not a uniform improvement with noise around it; it
is a set of subject-specific outcomes that differ in sign. The pooled
median of \qty{40}{\minute} describes the middle of that spread and
should not be read as an expected saving on a new target.

\paragraph{Reliability saturates where efficiency does not.}
On \emph{curl}, \emph{libxml2} and \emph{zstd} both corpora reproduced
the bug in all ten campaigns, so \rq{2.1} records no difference at all,
while the same subjects show \ac{RMST} differences between
\qty{1.5}{\hour} and \qty{3}{\hour} in \rq{2.3}. Where a bug is reachable
from either corpus, what augmentation changes is how quickly it is
reached, not whether it is reached. A study that measured only detection
rates at this budget would have reported no effect on precisely the
subjects where the effect is clearest.

\begin{figure}[p]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/coverage-rmst-bubble.pdf}
  \caption[]{Affected Coverage gained versus change in time-to-exposure, per project}
  \label{fig:aug_overhead}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Added coverage does not predict added benefit.}
Augmentation increased affected-line coverage across all twelve subjects by between \qty{0.6}{pp} and \qty{52.9}{pp}, yet the magnitude of that gain does not correlate with the magnitude of the speed-up. \Cref{fig:coverage-rmst-bubble} plots the two against each other, one bubble per project: the horizontal axis is the coverage the augmentation added over the corpus it started from (log-scaled, since the gains span two orders of magnitude), and the vertical axis is $\Delta\mathrm{RMST}$ over the \qty{8}{\hour} window --- the augmented arm's restricted mean survival time minus the original arm's --- so a project below the dashed line is one whose campaigns exposed the regression sooner once seeded with the augmented corpus. Bubble area encodes the corpus's \emph{total} affected coverage, marker shape the experiment set (circles tuning, squares held-out), and a hollow marker a project where neither arm reproduced the bug inside the window: its $\Delta\mathrm{RMST}$ is a difference of two censorings rather than a measured gap, and the four such projects are excluded from the correlation. Across the remaining eight the gain and the change in exposure time are essentially unrelated (Spearman $\rho = +\num{0.19}$, $p = \num{0.651}$, $n = \num{8}$): the two subjects that gained the most coverage, \emph{pcapplusplus} and \emph{ndpi}, sit almost exactly on the zero line, while the largest movement, \emph{yara}'s, followed a merely mid-range gain of \qty{9.1}{pp}. Whatever produced the occasional speed-up was not the quantity the augmentation optimizes for.

\paragraph{One result measures corpus construction, not fuzzing.}
\emph{Yara} is the largest effect in the study and the one that should carry the
least weight. Its augmented corpus already contained an input that crashed the
target before fuzzing started, so the \ac{RMST} of \num{0},seconds recorded for
it reflects how the corpus was built, not how the fuzzer searched. That input
was not a mutation of an existing seed. The agent had read the code change
that introduced the bug, and it produced a file that specifically exercised a
rarely used corner of the input format --- one for which that change had just
added new parsing code. None of the seeds in the original corpus used that part
of the format, so fuzzing from them would have had to construct the structure
from scratch before it could reach the new code at all. The result therefore
measures the agent acting on the diff, not the fuzzer benefiting from a
better starting point. It is kept in the tables because dropping a favorable
result after seeing it would bias the summary, but every claim about fuzzing
efficiency in this section holds without it, and \rq{2.2} is evaluated with it
set aside.

\paragraph{The largest loss is a corpus-construction effect too, not a search effect.}
\emph{Curl} is the one subject where the augmented corpus was slower by a margin
that survives correction, taking roughly an hour and a half longer to expose the
regression. The reason is visible in the corpus itself. This bug is reached only
along one particular interaction: the program has to follow a redirect from one
address to a second address and then re-encode that second address, and the
fault is in the re-encoding. An input that already performs that interaction is
most of the way to the bug, since the fuzzer only has to disturb the redirect
address; an input that does not has to build the interaction up by mutation
first. The original corpus contained many inputs that follow a redirect ---
roughly one in forty of its several thousand entries. The pipeline does not fuzz
from that corpus: it first reduces it to a small set chosen to cover the changed
code, then adds generated inputs. The reduction step cut the redirect-following
inputs from over a hundred to one, and the generator restored only about fifteen,
because producing a complete and valid interaction of this kind is far harder
than producing an input that merely reaches the changed lines once. The resulting
corpus covers the changed code better than the original did, yet leaves campaigns
far fewer starting points on the path to the bug, and their exposure times show
it: instead of the original's tight band around ten to fifteen minutes, the
augmented campaigns ranged from under two minutes to more than five hours.

\paragraph{Charged with its overhead, augmentation breaks even.}
Once the pipeline's own wall-clock cost is charged to each campaign, the median
paired difference falls to zero, and the split becomes even: at an \SI{8}{\hour}
budget, augmentation still buys time on some subjects, but on others, producing the
corpus costs about as much as the corpus saves. For most subjects, this cost is
modest. As depicted in \cref{fig:aug_overhead}, the median project spends \SI{78.5}{\minute} on corpus construction, about
\(16\%\) of the budget, and nine of the twelve stay below \SI{1.5}{\hour}. The
largest share of this is the LLM augmentation itself, which is bounded by the cycle
and token budget and takes between \SI{30}{\minute} and \SI{75}{\minute} on every
project. The remaining cost sits in a tail of three projects, curl, openssl and
poppler, whose overhead exceeds \SI{3}{\hour}. There, CoI computation and
distillation take \SIrange{2.4}{3.6}{\hour}, between \(74\%\) and \(88\%\) of each
project's overhead, and these three projects account for \(77\%\) of the time spent
on the two stages across all subjects.

Distillation in particular leaves substantial room for optimization. Our
implementation invokes \texttt{llvm-cov} once per seed and exports a separate
coverage report to disk before selecting the corpus that maximizes affected-code
coverage; retaining the coverage counters in memory and processing them directly
would remove the repeated report generation, serialization and file I/O. By
comparison, \texttt{afl-cmin}, which minimizes a corpus by edge coverage collected
through a fork server, never takes more than \SI{9.5}{\minute} on the full corpus in
our experiments, whereas our distillation exceeds it in 10 of the 12 projects, despite
processing only the seeds \texttt{afl-cmin} retains, and takes more than
\SI{1}{\hour} on curl, openssl and poppler.

\paragraph{What the sample can and cannot support.}
Overall, the results demonstrate that LLM-based corpus augmentation can substantially affect regression exposure time, but its benefit is target-dependent and not consistently positive. Across subjects, the observed reduction in \ac{RMST} is neither statistically significant nor robust to accounting for augmentation overhead. Moreover, increased affected-code coverage does not predict faster exposure, indicating that coverage of changed affected code alone is insufficient to explain the observed effects. The results therefore support augmentation as a potential target-specific optimization, but do not establish a general end-to-end advantage over the original corpus.
